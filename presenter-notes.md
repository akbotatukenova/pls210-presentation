# Presenter Notes — short guide, not a script

Target total speaking time: approximately **8.5–9.5 minutes**. Slides 2, 3, and 19 are mostly visual/transition screens.

## 1. Where do we draw the line? — ~25 sec
**Main idea:** Introduce boundary-drawing, not a verdict on cruelty.
**Say:** People can draw the line between demanding sport and unacceptable treatment differently. The project asks how they justify that line.
**Likely question:** Are you asking the audience to decide if the rider was cruel? → No. The prompt introduces the interpretive problem.

## 2. Drawing the Line — ~8 sec
**Main idea:** Name the project.
**Say:** My project studies how harm, cruelty, and tradition are negotiated in discussion of horse treatment at the World Nomad Games.
**Likely question:** Why “drawing the line”? → Because the object of study is how people construct boundaries between acceptable and unacceptable treatment.

## 3. The journey — ~8 sec
**Main idea:** Orient the audience.
**Say:** I move from the puzzle and question through the literature into my interpretive framework and expectations.
**Likely question:** None expected; move on quickly.

## 4. One controversy, competing meanings — ~35 sec
**Main idea:** Possible framings are the puzzle, not findings.
**Say:** The controversy may be framed through sport, risk, tradition, mistreatment, or cruelty. Tradition may also be used in more than one direction.
**Likely question:** Have you already found all these framings? → No. They are possible meanings to investigate, not established findings.

## 5. Research question and scope — ~45 sec
**Main idea:** Clarify both verbs and prevent representative claims.
**Say:** Interpretation is meaning assigned by an individual comment. Negotiation requires interaction in which meanings are defended, challenged, qualified, or reframed. My population is participants in selected public threads, not a wider national population.
**Likely question:** Why does “negotiate” require reply chains? → Because a reply must engage with the meaning or justification offered by another user.

## 6. Why interpretivism? — ~45 sec
**Main idea:** Meaning, not prevalence or veterinary diagnosis.
**Say:** I am asking what these categories mean to participants and how those meanings are contested in context. I am not estimating percentages or conducting a veterinary assessment.
**Likely question:** Does this mean physical harm is subjective? → No. The physical condition can exist independently; my research question is about the meaning people give it.

## 7. A focused extension — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Keep novelty modest.
**Say:** Cheung et al. already study social-media discourse around controversial horse treatment. My project asks more specifically how welfare judgments interact with tradition, authenticity, cultural legitimacy, and ideas of proper human–horse relations in this setting.
**Likely question:** What exactly do you add beyond Cheung et al.? → Tradition and cultural legitimacy are central objects of inquiry here, not merely the existence of online disagreement.

## 8. Where does sport end and abuse begin? — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Horse-welfare boundaries are contested.
**Say:** Cheung et al. and Graham & McManus show that practices can be framed differently as legitimate sporting activity or unacceptable treatment.
**Likely question:** Are these studies about the World Nomad Games? → No. They establish the broader conceptual problem, not evidence about my case.

## 9. Can using horses and caring for them coexist? — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Care and sporting use are not automatically opposites in participants' understandings.
**Say:** McManus & Montoya and Dashper complicate a simple sport-versus-welfare binary. Human–horse relations can involve care and partnership while still being shaped by human use and unequal power.
**Likely question:** Are you saying sport is caring? → No. I am saying participants may understand care and sporting use as coexisting; that relationship is analytically complicated.

## 10. What does tradition do? — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Tradition is not one fixed causal explanation.
**Say:** Sturød et al. show multiple cultural enactments of the horse in Kyrgyzstan. Hausberger et al. provides a bridge showing that social and cultural norms can shape welfare interpretation.
**Likely question:** Does Central Asian tradition accept cruelty? → That is exactly the simplistic claim I am avoiding. I am examining competing meanings of tradition and proper treatment.

## 11. Where the conversations meet — ~25 sec
**Main idea:** State the opening created by the literature.
**Say:** The literature establishes contested welfare boundaries, complicated care/use relations, and culturally situated meanings. My project asks how those layers interact in one controversy.
**Likely question:** Is this a “nobody studied my case” gap? → No. It is a conceptual combination and focused extension.

## 12. Sensitising concepts — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Four lenses guide attention without fixing findings.
**Say:** Harm/cruelty, tradition/authenticity, sporting risk, and care/respect tell me where to look. They are not boxes every comment must fit into.
**Likely question:** Why not call them coding categories? → Because I want them to remain revisable as participants introduce meanings I did not anticipate.

## 13. An open meaning-map — ~25 sec
**Main idea:** Show the lens visually and keep it open.
**Say:** The central interpretive question is what counts as acceptable horse treatment. The four concepts illuminate different ways users may construct that boundary, but new meanings can alter the framework.
**Likely question:** Are the lines causal? → No. They are visual relationships of attention, not causal arrows.

## 14. What I expect — and could be wrong about — ~40 sec
**Main idea:** Expectations are provisional, not hypotheses/findings.
**Say:** I expect tradition may work in more than one direction; harm and cruelty may be distinguished; and I will watch for disputes over who has authority to judge.
**Likely question:** Why include expectations in interpretivism? → Interpretive researchers do not enter without prior knowledge; the important thing is keeping expectations revisable rather than treating them as hypotheses to confirm.

## 15. Where I stand — ~45 sec
**Main idea:** Positionality creates insight and risk.
**Say:** I come from the region and the footage initially concerned me. That may help me recognize local references, but it may also make cruelty framings more salient to me. I will deliberately attend to interpretations that challenge my initial reaction.
**Likely question:** How will you prevent bias? → Not by pretending to be neutral, but by making my standpoint explicit and actively seeking disconfirming or alternative interpretations.

## 16. How AI and I worked together — ~30 sec
**Main idea:** Show intellectual co-working, not outsourcing.
**Say:** AI pressure-tested the question, challenged the tradition-versus-welfare binary, helped organize the literature, and forced me to narrow my contribution after Cheung et al. I made the final choices and checked sources.
**Likely question:** What part is yours? → The research question, final intellectual decisions, source verification, argument, and everything I am presenting and defending.

## 17. Clearer now — still unresolved — ~25 sec
**Main idea:** Genuine required reflection.
**Say:** I now understand the difference between interpretation and negotiation, how sensitising concepts work, and why interpretivism can still have expectations. Sampling breadth and multilingual interpretation remain unresolved.
**Likely question:** Why leave things unresolved? → Because this is Part 1 of the design; those are genuine later design decisions rather than facts I should invent now.

## 18. The line is the object of study — ~25 sec
**Main idea:** Return to the opening question.
**Say:** I am not trying to impose my own final line between sport, tradition, harm, and cruelty. I want to understand how participants draw and justify that line themselves.
**Likely question:** What would count as a successful answer? → A grounded interpretation of the meanings and boundary-making practices visible in the selected discussion, including contradictions and meanings I did not anticipate.

## 19. References — ~0–5 sec
**Main idea:** Sources are transparent and separated by type.
**Say:** Academic literature is separated from contextual reporting/event material.
**Likely question:** Which source is closest to your design? → Cheung, Mills & Ventura (2026).
